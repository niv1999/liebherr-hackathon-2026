Process model · Phase 1
Define Design Challenge
20 © Prof. Dr. Daniel Schallmo | Neu-Ulm University of Applied Sciences
Source: Based on Schallmo: Design Thinking erfolgreich anwenden, Springer Gabler
Phase 1: Define Design Challenge
OBJECTIVE
Allow a broad field of view and focus on a design challenge
ACTIVITIES
• Derivation and evaluation of different topics
• Formulation of a design challenge
• Determination of the project plan
RESULTS
• Topic pool with rated topic fields
• Design challenge with typical users
• Project plan with deadlines, costs and results
EXAMPLE: SUPERMARKET
The managing director of a supermarket has noticed that 
competition in food retail is increasing sharply and sales are 
stagnating or even declining. He and his team are looking for 
new services for selected customer segments. After deriving 
and discussing different topics, the team defines the design 
challenge: “How can we improve the shopping experience of 
older people in supermarkets?”

--- p23
Process model · Phase 2
Understand Design Challenge
21 © Prof. Dr. Daniel Schallmo | Neu-Ulm University of Applied Sciences
Source: Based on Schallmo: Design Thinking erfolgreich anwenden, Springer Gabler
Phase 2: Understand Design Challenge
OBJECTIVE
Ensure a common understanding, orientation towards users and 
building up specialist knowledge
ACTIVITIES
• Observation of typical users
• Interviewing typical users
• Testing of existing solutions
• Interviewing experts
• Gathering further information
RESULTS
• Observation protocol
• Interview protocol of typical users
• Test protocol of existing solutions
• Interview protocol of experts
• Evaluated information
EXAMPLE: SUPERMARKET
The team asks older people in the supermarket about their 
shopping experiences – with many open-ended questions; age 
and previous occupation are recorded. The results are 
summarized in interview protocols. The team also observes 
older people shopping and pays special attention to problems 
such as reaching products on upper or lower shelves or reading 
the ingredients on the packaging.

EXAMPLE: SUPERMARKET
Because of the social aspect, one team member has the idea of

Process model · Phase 5
Develop Prototypes
24 © Prof. Dr. Daniel Schallmo | Neu-Ulm University of Applied Sciences
Source: Based on Schallmo: Design Thinking erfolgreich anwenden, Springer Gabler
Phase 5: Develop Prototypes
OBJECTIVE
Convert relevant ideas into visible solutions
ACTIVITIES
• Development of different prototypes based on the relevant ideas
• Testing, improvement and combination of prototypes
RESULTS
• Prototypes in different versions (e.g. sketch, story, movie, model)
EXAMPLE: SUPERMARKET
Simple means such as tables (= counter), chairs (= seats) and foil 
(= treadmill) are used to visualize the ideas. Team members act 
out the roles of staff and users. This makes it clear, for 
example, that the treadmill must not be too far away from the 
user – otherwise the food is difficult to reach.

Prompting tips (1/2)
38 © Prof. Dr. Daniel Schallmo | Neu-Ulm University of Applied Sciences
Six tips for effective prompts
TIP 1
Be clear and specific
State clearly what you want; ambiguous 
prompts lead to unexpected results.
TIP 2
Provide context
Include relevant details so the model 
understands the scenario.
TIP 3
Specify the format
Say whether you want a list, a paragraph, a 
table or code.
TIP 4
Use examples
Examples show the kind of information or 
style you are looking for.
TIP 5
Experiment and iterate
If the first prompt fails, try different wording 
or approaches.
TIP 6
Start with a seed
A seed sentence sets the tone and guides the 
direction.

TIP 7
Control the output length

Prompt catalogue for Design Thinking
43 © Prof. Dr. Daniel Schallmo | Neu-Ulm University of Applied Sciences
AI supports the process – but never replaces real users
PURPOSE
• Use AI productively throughout the Design Thinking process
• AI supports ideation, structuring, reflection and critique – but never 
replaces user research, team discussion or validation
• All AI-generated output must be critically reviewed, adapted and 
validated
HOW TO USE AI CRITICALLY
• Generate with AI, then validate with evidence
• Distinguish evidence-based findings, plausible assumptions and 
unsupported claims
• Challenge bias, oversimplification and missing perspectives
• Document what was generated with AI and what was validated 
through observation, interviews, tests or expert input

--- p49
Excursus
Giving feedback
46 © Prof. Dr. Daniel Schallmo | Neu-Ulm University of Applied Sciences
Formulate descriptively – not personally judgmental
PROCEDURE
1 Take notes during the presentation: what did I like, what can be done better, open 
questions?
2 Only record behaviour that everyone can understand
3 Ask whether the person is open to feedback
4 Describe the overall impression positively
5 Move from the positive to the negative
6 Use the “sandwich”: wrap a negative comment in two positive ones
7 Add a suggestion for improvement to each criticism
BASIC FORMULATION RULES
• Always from the “I” perspective: “I feel that 
…”
• Descriptive, constructive, concrete
• Not only negative
• Do not mention anything irreversible
