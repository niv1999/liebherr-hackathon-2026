Abstract: The purpose of this paper is to clarify the definition of 
Design Thinking and to introduce a structured approach with phases, 
activities and results. Our research is based on a literature review 
giving insight into the basic understanding of Design Thinking and on 
existing approaches. The main findings are that although Design 
Thinking is a widely known concept an approach for a structured 
development of new solutions a clear definition and approach is 
missing. This paper offers an integrated approach for Design Thinking 
including basic principles and a Roadmap.  
Keywords: Design Thinking, integrated approach, phases and steps, 
tools.  
 
1 Introduction 
The consistent orientation of solutions to customers’ requirements and needs is a key 
success factor for today’s companies. But who are the customers? Who are the users? 
More importantly, how can requirements and needs be identified and implemented in 
customer-oriented solutions?   
 
2 
 
 
Design Thinking is an approach that helps to answer exactly these questions. In our 
contribution, the approach of Design Thinking is explained. A seven-phase Roadmap will 
also be presented, each containing objectives, activities, tools, and examples. 
At the beginning of the 1960s, it was discovered that collaboration in creative processes 
between designers, engineers, and representatives of other disciplines was made more 
difficult, as there was often a different educational background and thus conflicting 
approaches to solving problems. Intuitively, extensive questions were subdivided into sub 
questions without grounded theories (Plattner et al., 2009, P. 60).  
Partial solutions were then developed for the partial questions, which were then 
combined to form an overall solution. The result was the design process which was used 
as a common process. The design process includes not only a solution search but also a 
clear formulation of the problems or questions to be solved. This makes it possible to 
create a common starting point for all participants, even from different disciplines. This 
common starting point then serves to jointly generate new knowledge and thus develop 
new solutions.  
The design process includes iterations to improve the solutions and increase knowledge. 
Furthermore, the process is characterized by analytical and synthetic phases (Plattner et 
al, 2009, p. 60). 
Based on the literature, Design Thinking has been discussed and applied for several years 
but our work contributes to existing knowledge of Design Thinking by developing an 
integrated approach with clear steps and tools which is applicable for companies.  
In our research, we first focus on existing approaches for Design Thinking by analyzing 
the existing literature. Based on this, we provide an integrated approach that consist of 
seven phases with objectives, activities, tools and relevant results. The integrated 
approach has been applied in academia and practice in order to ensure a validity and 
relevance. 
Our contribution is significant to give an overview on existing approaches for Design 
Thinking. Based on these insights, we deliver our integrated approach for Design 
Thinking.

There is currently no commonly accepted definition for the term Design Thinking. 
Selected definitions in the context of the Design Thinking are shown in Table 1.

These definitions all contain the following primary aspects: 
• Setting Goals: The goal of Design Thinking is to develop new solution for 
existing or future problems. 
• Orientation: Design Thinking is geared specifically towards the users. 
• Process: Design Thinking takes place on the basis of structured and iterative 
processes. 
• Participants: Design Thinking should utilize interdisciplinary teams. 
 
Based on the literature, we propose the following definition of Design Thinking for our 
research: 
 
The approach of Design Thinking pursues the objective of developing new 
solutions for existing problems. These solutions are consistently oriented 
towards the needs of users and have a positive influence. The Design Thinking 
process is structured and iterative. Within the Design Thinking process, a 
multidisciplinary team uses techniques. 
Existing Approaches 
There exist several approaches for Design Thinking within the literature (Brown, 2008; 
Ideo, 2012; Plattner et al., 2009; d.school, 2010; Liedtka & Ogilvie, 2011) that addresses 
Design Thinking in a structured way. 
Tim Brown introduced the Ideo’s Design Thinking approach in his seminal article in the 
Harvard Business Review. He outlined examples and sketched the Design Thinking  
 
4 
 
 
process. In an additional contribution, the Ideo’s approach was further explored 
specifically in the educational context which the insights gained was incorporated into his 
own approach. 
Plattner, Meinel, and Weinberg’s approach outlines vital advancements in the Design 
Thinking field and was used at the Hasso Plattner Institute in Potsdam and at Stanford 
University in Palo Alto (2009). 
The d.school’s approach (2010) exhibits similarities to Plattner et al.‘s approach; 
therefore a partnership was formed between the two institutes. 
Liedtka and Ogilvie‘s (2011) approach consists a procedure consisting of four phases and 
ten techniques which were described in detail. Their approach was developed based on 
the authors’ practical teaching and consulting experiences  
Although the existing approaches make valuable contributions, they do not cover a clear 
picture of Design Thinking and do not deliver an integrated approach with objectives, 
activities, tools and results. In our contribution, we initiate the basic principles and 
Roadmap for Design Thinking including several phases. 
3 Research Questions and Research Design 
Research questions 
In the previous section, our brief literature review provided a theoretical foundation of 
Design Thinking. To serve as a basis for our Roadmap, four existing approaches for 
Design Thinking were individually selected.  
Based on the problem described and current understanding, we attempt to answer the 
following main research questions: 
• What approaches exist for Design Thinking and how can they be described in a 
structured way? 
• What are basic principles for Design Thinking? 
• How does an integrated approach for Design Thinking look like? 
• What are the relevant phases for Design Thinking?  
Research Design 
In our research, we applied literature review and case study research methods to complete 
the literature review. Case study research (Benbasat et al., 1987; Eisenhardt, 1989; Yin, 
2013; Stake, 1995) analyses relevant cases, describes them and develops empirically 
grounded concepts (Kromrey, 2009).  
We conducted the literature review to gain insight into current research in Design 
Thinking. Based on this, we developed an integrated approach for Design Thinking.  
  
4 Literature Review on Existing Approaches 
Brown's approach (2008) 
Tim Brown presents Ideo's approach to Design Thinking in a Harvard Business Review 
article. He shows examples and outlines the process of Design Thinking. Another article 
further explains an Ideo’s approach which is suitable for use in education and its findings 
are incorporated into the development of its own approach (Ideo, 2012). 
Principles 
Brown does not explicitly explain the principles of Design Thinking, but makes 
recommendations on how Design Thinking can be used within companies (2008, p. 90): 
• Integration of Design Thinkers: Design Thinkers should be involved in the 
process right from the start. 
• User-centricity: Orientation towards the needs, preferences, and behavior of 
users makes it possible to gain new insights and align solutions to them. 
• Performing experiments: Experiments should be carried out early and often. 
• Integrating external knowledge: Users should be integrated into the 
development. 
• Track projects of any size: Both incremental, short-term projects revolutionary 
and long-term concepts should be implemented. 
• Definition of budgets: Design Thinking can be a fast and meaningful result 
available in a short time that should be marketed. A suitable budget must be 
available for this purpose. 
• Building interdisciplinary teams: To this end, employees should be sought at 
suitable universities. 
• Run through the process completely: Only if the process is completed can the 
hoped-for results be achieved. 
Procedure model   
Brown proposes three phases (2008, p. 88 f) which are shown in Figure 1 and explained 
below. 
 
Figure 1 Ideo’s Procedure model (Brown, 2008, p. 88) 
 
Inspiration: Understanding the environment with its problems and opportunities 
 
 
6 
 
 
In the first phase, the problem is defined, opportunities and changes identified and users 
and their environment observed. Existing restrictions within one’s own company are 
taken into consideration. Different disciplines are remotely linked. 
Brainstorming: Acquisition, development, and testing of ideas 
Brainstorming sessions and sketches are carried out within the idea generation process. It 
must be ensured that customer-oriented work is carried out and that prototypes are 
constantly tested and further developed. Users are also involved in the tests.  
Implementation: Preparation for introduction   
In the final phase, the definitive prototype is implemented. For this purpose, a 
communication strategy is defined and a business case is created. 
The phases shown are not linear but interconnected. 
Techniques and Results 
Techniques and results are not explained by Brown. However, Ideo's approach to 
teaching includes numerous techniques. These include, for example, questionnaire 
structure, field research, expert interviews, storytelling, brainstorming (Ideo, 2012, p. 40). 
Summary 
Ideo's approach shows a three-phase procedure model. Recommendations are also given 
on how Design Thinking can be used within companies. Techniques are not shown. 
 
Plattner, Meinel, and Weinberg’s Approach (2009) 
Plattner, Meinel and Weinberg's approach represents an experience report on Design 
Thinking and has already been used at the Hasso-Plattner-Institute in Potsdam and at 
Stanford University in Palo Alto (2009. p. 8). 
Principles 
Plattner et al. discuss principles in two places, which are briefly explained below (2009, 
p. 104 and p. 127): 
• Multidisciplinary teams as a necessity for the success of Design Thinking, as the 
spectrum of expertise, is more diversified. 
• Not only specialists, because they already know the solution and often reach 
their limits. 
• Consideration of different learning styles to promote the Design Thinking 
process. 
• Open space with mobile furniture and attractive furnishings to encourage 
creativity and teamwork.  
• Repetition in the form of iterations to learn from mistakes and thus take 
advantage of the opportunity to bring about improvement. A reiteration is not 
seen as a loss of time, but as a gain in knowledge. 
• Visualize to make ideas visible in images. Texts and notes are linked with 
photos, videos, sketches, drawings, and symbols. 
• Use brainstorming in accordance with certain rules to develop ideas and 
solutions. 
• Consideration of time to quickly develop ideas. The definition of time 
timeframes does not prevent the generation of ideas but promotes spontaneity. 
Procedure model 
Plattner proposes six phases (p. 113 ff), which are shown in Figure 2 and explained 
below. 
 
Figure 2 Procedure model of Plattner et al. (Plattner et al. 2009, p. 113) 
 
Understand: Understanding the problem and its environment 
This phase describes the task and defines the problem. It represents the most important 
phase in the procedure model since errors that occur here affect the entire process. The 
Design Challenge describes the task to be solved with the process. The focus is on user 
orientation and the question of the target group. Depending on the Design Challenge, a 
combined understanding should be created within multidisciplinary teams. 
Observe: Observe concerned users 
In the second phase, the members of the team must acquire expert knowledge for the task 
at hand. Existing solutions are analyzed and questioned. Following the precise definition 
of the users, they are observed and interviewed personally. In the context of observation, 
the use of products and behaviors must be analyzed.  
Point of View: Understanding the problem world 
The knowledge gained is evaluated, interpreted and weighted. The experiences of the 
entire team are summarized in order to create a common basis. A typical, fictitious person 
is created, which is described holistically. It is important to separate relevant facts from 
non-relevant facts. 
Ideate: Develop, compile and evaluate solutions 
Brainstorming generates ideas that contain a solution. Following the acquisition of 
numerous ideas, they are evaluated, selected and described. 
Prototypes: Create comprehensible solutions for the user. 
 
 
8 
 
 
By developing prototypes, selected ideas are to be made visible and communicated in 
order to receive feedback from users. Prototypes do not have to be extremely expensive 
or perfect. 
Test: Trying out solutions together with users 
The prototypes developed are tested by design thinkers and users to learn about the 
strengths and weaknesses of an idea and learn from the results. 
The phases shown are iteratively interlinked; feedback is made therefore possible in order 
to refine the findings and make adjustments. Once the test phase is completed, the Design 
Thinking process is also completed and the technical, social and economic feasibility is 
clarified. If feasibility is not achievable, the point of view is redefined and the process is 
repeated 
Techniques and Outcomes 
Techniques and results are not explained in detail but extensively mentioned. Techniques 
include market research techniques (interviews and observation), persona, storytelling, 
role-playing, and brainstorming. 
Summary 
The approach of Plattner et al (2009) shows basic principles and a procedure model with 
six phases, which are deposited with exemplary projects (p. 163). Techniques are 
mentioned, but not explained in detail, as they are usually known. In addition, there are 
findings on the development of Design Thinking and on the personal characteristics of 
Design Thinkers, which have already been explained in the theoretical principles of this 
book. 
 
d. school’s Approach (2010) 
The approach of each d. school in Stanford is similar to the approach of Plattner et al. as 
there is a current cooperation.  
Principles 
The principles of the d. school’s approach are called mindset and are briefly explained 
below (2010, p. 3): 
• Activity: The activity is more important than holding official meetings. 
• Experiment: Experiments and prototypes are part of the entire process and are 
used to gain experience. 
• Empathy: The Empathy for users is important to develop suitable solutions. 
• Visualization: The Visualization serves to communicate ideas. 
• Transparency: A common vision is formulated from different problems.  
• Procedure: The techniques, the goals, and the position must be clear within the 
process. 
• Diversification: Collaboration of team members with different backgrounds.  
Procedure model 
The procedure model of the d. school is characterized by five phases, which are shown in 
Figure 18 and explained below (2010, p: 4). 
Empathize 
During the first phase, users and their behavior are observed. Furthermore, they are 
questioned and their own experiences with existing products and services are gained. The 
objective is to build an understanding of the users and their problems. 
 
Figure 3 Procedure model of the d. school (based on d. school, 2010, p. 4) 
 
Define 
The insights that were gained in the previous phase are summarized into concrete needs 
in order to formulate a relevant challenge on this basis. The objective is to develop a 
more definite understanding of users than in the previous phase and, building on this, to 
define a common position on the challenge. 
Ideate 
In this phase, ideas are gained for the formulated challenge. The objective is to derive 
many different Ideas for the next phase. 
Prototype 
The creation of prototypes makes it possible to turn ideas into tangible solutions. 
Prototypes can be represented as an exemplary role plays or as models. Prototypes are 
helpful when the team and users can try them out and gain experience. 
Test 
The prototypes created are tested in this phase to make improvements. The tests take 
place in the context of the users. The tests also provide an opportunity to gain further 
information about the user and to change the formulation of the challenge if necessary. 
Analogous to the approach of Plattner et al., the phases of the d. school are iteratively 
connected.  
Techniques and Outcomes 
The approach of d. school contains numerous techniques that are assigned to the 
 
 
10 
 
 
described phases. The techniques are described with the reasons for the use and the 
procedure. The techniques include; Assumption of a Beginners Mindset, User Camery 
Study and Extreme Users (d. school, 2010, p. 9). 
Summary 
The approach of d. school (2010) shows basic principles and a procedures model with 
five phases. Techniques are available for the phases, which are explained with their 
reasons and the procedure.  
 
Liedtka & Ogilvie’s Approach (2011) 
The approach described by Liedtka and Ogilvie includes an approach consisting of four 
phases and ten techniques, which are described in detail. It is based on the experiences of 
the two authors who have gained them in teaching and consulting.  
Principles 
Do not show any explicit principles of Design Thinking within individual techniques, for 
example, Brainstorming, however, some principles are presented in this regard (Liedtka 
& Ogilvie, 2011, p. 104). 
Procedure model 
Liedtka and Ogilvie propose four phases, which are shown in Figure 4 and explained 
below (2011, p. 21): 
 
Figure 4 Liedtka & Ogilvie’s Procedure model (based on Liedtka & Ogilvie, 2011) 
 
What is? Analysis of the Current Situation 
In this phase, the current situation and problems of users are analyzed. The aim is to find 
out what frustrates users and what disturbs them in their current situation. Journey 
Mapping builds an understanding of users to assess the potential of a new idea. Value 
chain analysis is used to check whether your company is capable of implementing the 
new idea and uncovering gaps. Using mind mapping, all information gained is structured 
in order to make it available in the next phase. 
What if? Shaping the future 
In the second phase, new possibilities and trends are taken into account in order to derive 
future developments and ideas. Brainstorming is used to winning ideas. Ideas are then 
grouped and described in the form of concepts. 
 
What wows? Making of Decisions 
The concepts described are tested within this phase with assumptions in order to 
concentrate on the most promising concepts. Prototypes are created for the promising 
concepts, which are then tested with users and partners. 
What works? Marketing 
The last phase serves to work on the prototypes and to develop them further. Market 
introduction is planned for the improved prototypes and further information is obtained. 
During these phases, divergent and convergent thinking is used in different ways. In the 
first two phases of the procedure model, a broad field of vision is opened up (divergent) 
in order to detach oneself from existing solution approaches. In the last two phases of the 
procedure model, the focus is then placed on the promising options (convergent).  
Techniques 
There are ten techniques that are explained in detail and assigned to the four phases. The 
techniques include (Liedtka & Ogilvie, 2011, p.45): 
• Visualization: Transformation of information into pictures, sketches, narratives 
etc. 
• Journey Mapping: Representation of the interaction of the user, while this 
contains products or services of the company. 
• Value Chain Analysis: Analysis of the company's interactions with partners is 
necessary to create, market and distribute services. 
• Mind Mapping: Structuring of a large amount of information that was 
previously collected. 
• Brainstorming: Gaining ideas, especially alternatives to the status quo. 
• Concept Development: Selection of the best ideas for brainstorming, description 
as detailed concepts and evaluation based on users and company criteria. 
• Assumption Testing: Examination of assumptions regarding the described 
concepts and selection of promising concepts. 
• Rapid Prototyping: prototypes are created for selected concepts. Drawings, 
functional prototypes and market test prototypes can be created. 
• Customer Co-Creation: Integration of potential users into the development of 
service offerings. 
• Learning Launch: open assumptions about the range of services are tested on the 
market in the form of an experiment.  
In addition to these techniques, the authors present four tools that are used within a 
Design Thinking project at the beginning of each phase (Liedtka & Ogilvie, 2011, p.35). 
• Design Note: Description of the project with goals, resources, deadlines, etc. 
• Design Criteria: Criteria for evaluating alternative designs; includes user needs  
 
12 
 
 
and company requirements. 
• Napkin Pitch: Description and communication of solution concepts in detail. 
• Learning Guide: Determining the use of resources to gain further experience. 
Summary 
Liedtka and Ogilvie’s approach contains a procedure model with four phases and 
techniques that are assigned to the respective phases. There are also four tools in the form 
of formulas to be completed at the beginning of a phase. 
 
5 Integrated approach for Design Thinking  
In the following section we describe our integrated approach which consists of basic 
principles and the Roadmap. 
Basic Principles 
Design Thinking is characterized by four basic principles: 1. human needs as a starting 
point, 2. Multidisciplinary teams; 3. Iterative process and 4. Creative environment. The 
four basic principles are shown in figure 5. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Figure 5 Basic Principles of Design Thinking 
 
Humans as a starting point 
Within Design Thinking, people are the source of inspiration for new ideas. The human 
being and his needs are in the foreground. Once the requirements have been determined, 
the next step is to check which products and services are technically feasible. Then it is 
checked which solutions are economical. Since a fundamental orientation towards people 
and their needs takes place, firstly less extensive marketing is necessary and secondly the 
flop rate of new products and services is lower (Weinberg, 2012; d. school, 2010; Plattner 
et al., 2009.; Brown, 2008).  
Multidisciplinary Teams 
The second principle is the use of multidisciplinary teams. The focus here is not on the 
creative capabilities of individuals, but on the creative capabilities of interdisciplinary 
teams with four to six participants. Care is taken to ensure that 50% of the teams are 
made up of men and 50% women. It must also be ensured that the disciplines are equally 
represented (Weinberg, 2012; Plattner, 2009; d. school, 2010; Brown, 2008).   
Each member of a multidisciplinary team is called a Design Thinker. The personal 
characteristics of a Design Thinker play a major role and are explained below (Plattner et 
al., 2009).  
• Optimism: A Design Thinker should be optimistic about his ability to develop 
better solutions with Design Thinking. Optimism is common and coupled with 
enthusiasm.  
• Empathy: A Design Thinker should have a high degree of empathy to see the 
world through the eyes of the user and to understand his perception and 
sensation. Furthermore, the reasons for a certain perception should be analyzed 
in order to identify unfulfilled wishes and needs of users.  
• Integrative Thinking: A Design Thinker should be able to analyze products, 
processes, and systems holistically in order to detect both obvious and hidden 
defects. In addition, it is necessary either to select the best solution from existing 
solutions, to develop new solutions or to change existing solutions in such a way 
that better results are achieved. 
• Experimentation: A Design Thinker should enjoy trying new things, gaining 
experience and taking risks, making mistakes and learning from them. 
Assumptions should also be made and tested.  
• Cooperation capability: A Design Thinker should be cooperative because 
complex problems require joint work on solutions. The joint work with people 
from other disciplines is also relevant here.   
Iterative process 
Due to the use of multidisciplinary teams, all participants have different work processes 
due to their training and experience. For this reason, it is necessary to provide a process 
that combines analytic with intuition and is understandable for all participants. Iterations 
are possible within the process in order to improve the solutions.  
 
14 
 
 
In addition to the iteration of the process, it is characterized by a variety of divergent and 
convergent thinking. In the first three phases, a broad field of vision is opened (divergent) 
in order to gain a lot of input and to concentrate on a few perspectives at the end of the 
third phase. Ideas for solutions are then generated and prototypes are developed 
(divergent) in order to focus on one after testing prototypes that are integrated into a 
business model (convergent). Furthermore, the Design Thinking process leads to ever 
more concrete results, which is represented by the yellow line (Liedtka & Ogilvie, 2011; 
Ideo, 2012a).  
In some phases of the Design Thinking process, brainstorming is used, for which certain 
rules are relevant (Ideo 2012; d. school, 2010): 
• Visualize: Problems, ideas, and solution approach are to be visualized by means 
of sketches. 
• Only one speaks: only one Design Thinker speaks at a time, as all ideas should 
be heard. 
• Promote ideas: unusual ideas should be promoted, even if they initially appear 
unrealistic. 
• Postpone critique: there are no bad ideas and there is enough time to elaborate 
them. 
• Experiment: experiment throughout the process to develop further ideas and 
identify weaknesses. 
• Track activity: Activity in the form of actions is relevant. 
• Quantity is important: in order to find a good idea, many ideas have to be won. 
• Focus: all team members should stick to the topic and not lose sight of the main 
goal. 
• Building on ideas: derived ideas should be further developed; better "and" than 
"but". 
• Turn off electronic devices: all team members participate and focus on the 
process.  
Creative Environment 
The described process takes place in a creative environment that is characterized by an 
idea-promoting division and institution. 
In addition to the work environment shown, rooms for workshops can be redesigned to 
support creative work. In addition, materials that can be used in the Design Thinking 
process are also relevant.   
Roadmap 
A Roadmap is given here based on the presented approaches to Design Thinking and 
based on existing theories about Design Thinking, the Roadmap for Design Thinking is 
explained as follows: 
• Defining Design Challenge: First of all, it is necessary to derive different 
subject areas. The different subject areas are discussed, in order to then commit 
oneself to one particular subject area. A Design Challenge is formulated for the 
selected topic area, which is to be answered. Typical users are also defined. 
Subsequently, a project plan is drawn up, which includes deadlines, costs, and 
results.  
• Understanding Design Challenge: In this phase, it is important to build a 
common understanding of the Design Challenge to be accomplished. For this 
purpose, typical users are analyzed in relevant situations and with the problems 
they have to cope with. This analysis is carried out by means of a survey and 
observation. Existing solutions that are available on the market are tested and 
experts are interviewed to build expertise on the Design Challenge. 
• Defining Perspectives: In this phase, it is important to build a common 
understanding of the Design Challenge to be accomplished. For this purpose, 
typical users are analyzed in relevant situations and with the problems they have 
to cope with. This analysis is carried out by means of a survey and observation. 
Existing solutions that are available on the market are tested and experts are 
interviewed to build expertise of the Design Challenge.  
• Gaining Ideas: In this phase, ideas are gained through the use of creativity 
techniques that are intended to meet the previously identified needs. These ideas 
are grouped and revised. The ideas are then described and evaluated.  
• Developing Prototypes: The ideas gained serve to develop prototypes. A 
prototype thus represents a solution to the challenge described. Prototypes can 
be developed in different forms. Following the development of prototypes, these 
prototypes are improved and combined.  
• Testing Prototypes: The different prototypes are presented to the users and then 
tested with them. The aim is to gain important experience, also with regard to 
the use of prototypes. Prototypes are also tested in the market. The experience 
gained is then used to improve and further develop the prototypes. Based on the 
evaluation of the prototypes, a promising prototype is selected.  
• Integrating Prototypes: In this phase, a uniform grid is used to develop a 
business model that integrates the prototype for a product or service. Thus, a 
business model with the following five dimensions is available: customer 
dimension, user dimension, value-added dimension, partner dimension, financial 
dimension.

6 Contribution 
The contribution of this research is initiating the discussion of Design Thinking by 
providing a literature review, a concise definition and basic principles for Design 
Thinking. Based on this, we presented an integrated seven-step approach on how to 
successfully apply Design Thinking. This addresses an existing research gap regarding 
design thinking, which is linked to innovation management in general.  
7 Practical Implications 
Senior managers and business developers will gain from our findings by acquiring a clear 
definition and the basic principles of Design Thinking. The seven-step Roadmap enables 
companies to take advantage of Design Thinking’s potential. By applying the Roadmap 
in a proper way companies are enabled to optimize their current solutions (mainly 
products and services) and create a distinct competitive advantage by providing new 
ideas at a lower risk and within a shorter time to the market.  
8 Limitations 
The aim of this paper is to report our research results on Design Thinking. The reader 
should bear in mind that due to practical constraints, the results may not be generalized. 
Furthermore, some aspects (e.g. broad application of our approach in several industries) 
needs to be further investigated. Therefore, we recommend interviews with practitioners 
and longitudinal studies in other circumstances in order to establish a greater 
understanding of Design Thinking. 
9 Recommendations for Further Research 
Further research regarding the impact of Design Thinking would be worthwhile. For 
example, it would be interesting to create a knowledge building community where 
researchers and practitioners can compare experiences gained from our approach in 
different industries and company sizes. Another possible area of future research would be 
to investigate the role of Design Thinking within innovation management, particularly in 
the field of business model innovation. Lastly, future studies need to provide quantifiable 
benefits of Design Thinking.
